## Preamble: What This Proposal Carries

This document is a proposal for federal investment in a sovereign domestic materials economy — one rooted in American soil, recharged by American snowmelt and monsoon rain, moved by American rail, and built by American workers earning living wages through a federally guaranteed employment program.

The economy this proposal describes produces food, fuel, rubber, bioplastic film, insulation, and construction materials from plants that already grow in the watersheds where the people live. It extends growing seasons in cold climates through greenhouse infrastructure built from the same plant-derived materials. It pays for itself through the productive capacity of the land — through harvests that compound year over year as the soil deepens, the cooperatives mature, and the rail corridor becomes a supply chain rather than a coal corridor transitioning toward dormancy.

The proposal applies Modern Monetary Theory as its funding architecture. The federal government of a sovereign currency-issuing nation faces no revenue constraint of the kind a household faces. The binding constraints on public investment are labor, materials, and ecological capacity. This program addresses all three by employing willing workers at living wages, cultivating materials from renewable biological systems, and operating within the ecological discipline of watershed-scale stewardship. The real question Congress faces is never whether we can afford it. The question is whether we have the will to organize the work.

We believe we do.

## Part One: The Condition We Are Responding To

### I. The Supply Chain Signal

In late February 2026, a military conflict in the Middle East disrupted the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which roughly one-fifth of the world's petroleum supply normally flows. Oil prices rose sharply within days. Fertilizer prices, which depend on petroleum-based production processes, are projected to double before the 2026 planting season ends. Greenhouse film, hoop house covering, agricultural plastic mulch, synthetic rubber components for farm equipment — all of these depend on the same petroleum feedstock chain that the Strait disruption placed under pressure.

Every farm that grows food through living soil — through biological nitrogen fixation, through perennial root systems, through the patient relationship between plants and fungi and the microorganisms that build organic matter — grew just as productively this spring as it did last spring. Every food forest that draws its water from snowmelt, its fertility from composted plant matter, and its fuel from canola pressed on-site stands on the same ground it always stood on. The disruption revealed what was already true: the food and materials systems that work with nature hold steady when distant supply chains do not.

This is the signal the present proposal responds to. The clarity it brings is a gift.

### II. The Petroleum Dependency Map

United States petroleum consumption runs approximately 20 million barrels per day. Motor gasoline accounts for roughly 43 percent — personal and commercial ground vehicles. Distillate fuel oil and diesel account for approximately 20 percent — trucks, trains, tractors, construction equipment. Petrochemical feedstocks for plastics and synthetic materials account for approximately 18 percent. The remaining fraction covers jet fuel, asphalt, lubricants, and industrial chemicals.

The food system's petroleum dependency lives primarily in processing, packaging, and distribution — roughly 80 percent of food energy use — rather than on the farm itself. On-farm agriculture accounts for under 2 percent of total U.S. primary energy use. The kitchen refrigerator uses more energy than the farm tractor. This means the path to agricultural petroleum independence runs through the packaging, the fuel, the building materials, and the supply chain infrastructure — not primarily through the crop itself.

The present proposal targets each of these layers directly: ecodiesel from canola for transportation fuel, PHA bioplastic film from canola glycerin for greenhouse covering and packaging, guayule natural rubber from the American Southwest for structural sealing components and durable tarps, and hempcrete from domestic hemp fiber for insulated greenhouse walls and food forest shelter buildings. Together these four material streams replace petroleum inputs across the full agricultural infrastructure chain.

### III. The Water and Rail Infrastructure Already in Place

The watersheds this proposal builds upon share three properties that make them the right foundation for a sovereign materials economy.

First, their water arrives by gravity. The Flathead River drains the Cabinet and Swan Range glaciers into the Flathead Valley of northwestern Montana. Lake Pend Oreille in northern Idaho holds 44 million acre-feet of glacially carved freshwater, fed by the Clark Fork River's entire western Montana drainage, with the Spokane Valley-Rathdrum Prairie Aquifer supplying half a million people beneath the valley floor without a single petroleum-powered pumping station drawing from distant sources. The Champlain Basin in Vermont and New York receives two trillion gallons of inflow annually from the Green Mountains and the Adirondacks. The Big Horn Mountains of northern Wyoming deliver snowmelt recharge to the alluvial aquifers of Sheridan County. The acequias of New Mexico carry water by gravity from mountain snowmelt through desert communities whose water-sharing wisdom predates European presence on this continent by centuries.

Second, freight rail already serves every node. The BNSF Empire Builder runs daily through Whitefish, Montana and Sandpoint, Idaho on its Chicago-to-Seattle route. BNSF and Union Pacific both serve Sheridan, Wyoming and connect north to Billings, Montana. The Amtrak Vermonter connects Burlington, Vermont to New York City daily. BNSF and Union Pacific traverse the Rio Grande corridor of New Mexico and Arizona. The Palouse canola ecodiesel corridor of eastern Washington and northern Idaho sits eighty miles south of Sandpoint along the same BNSF line. These rail connections are not future investments — they are existing infrastructure ready to carry sovereign materials today.

Third, cooperative agricultural organization is already present in every watershed. Vermont's food cooperatives, CSA networks, and farmers markets represent the most developed community food sovereignty infrastructure in New England. Montana's Flathead Valley hosts active food forest agriculture and community grain growing. Wyoming's food sovereignty organizations received state food security funding in 2025. New Mexico's acequia associations are among the oldest continuously operating cooperative water and agricultural governance structures in North America. The social infrastructure for cooperative production exists. This proposal funds the physical infrastructure to match it.

## Part Two: The Four Material Systems

### IV. Canola Ecodiesel — The Fuel That Powers Everything Else

Canola ecodiesel is diesel fuel pressed from oilseed canola and chemically converted through transesterification into a fuel that runs in any standard diesel engine without modification, at any blend ratio from five percent to one hundred percent pure biodiesel.

The European Union has operated a mature canola biodiesel system for over two decades. Canola oil accounts for nearly 40 percent of all EU biodiesel feedstock. Germany alone cultivates over 1.1 million hectares of winter canola annually. Independent lifecycle analyses document that canola biodiesel reduces greenhouse gas emissions by up to 70 percent compared to fossil diesel. This is proven, working infrastructure operating at continental scale, funding models available for direct adaptation.

The United States canola geography concentrates in North Dakota — approximately 80 percent of domestic production — with strong secondary corridors in Montana, Washington, Idaho, and Minnesota. The Grain OS program proposes a Job Guarantee-funded canola expansion corridor anchored in the Palouse region of eastern Washington and northern Idaho, with secondary cultivation in the Flathead Valley of Montana, the Big Horn Basin of Wyoming, and the Vermont-Champlain corridor.

The Grain OS position on GMO canola is clear and ecologically grounded: all ecodiesel production uses non-GMO, open-pollinated, regionally adapted varieties. GMO canola pollen is wind-borne and travels long distances; genetic contamination of non-GMO seed supplies is documented as pervasive within seven years of GMO introduction in Canada. The contamination risk is identical regardless of end use — the pollen does not distinguish a fuel field from a food field. Non-GMO canola is fully viable at commercial yield; Clearfield varieties developed through traditional plant breeding deliver herbicide tolerance without genetic modification.

The canola press cake — the protein-rich solid remaining after oil extraction — returns to the food forest as compost amendment, cycling fertility back to the soil. The glycerin byproduct of transesterification — currently a low-value waste stream at most ecodiesel facilities — becomes the primary feedstock for the PHA bioplastic fermentation cooperative described in the next section. Nothing leaves the cooperative as waste.

### V. PHA Bioplastic Film — The Greenhouse Covering That Composts

Polyhydroxyalkanoate bioplastic, known as PHA, is produced by bacteria that consume fermentable sugars and excrete a flexible, compostable polymer as their metabolic byproduct. PHA is already listed in the horticultural engineering literature as a recognized greenhouse covering material alongside polyethylene, PVC, and polycarbonate. PHA greenhouse films offer excellent light transmission, thermal insulation, and durability, creating optimal growing conditions for plants while reducing energy consumption. PHA is fully biodegradable in soil, marine, and compost environments — meaning the greenhouse film that wears out after four to five years of service composts into the same soil the greenhouse protects, with no microplastic residue.

The fermentation process runs most efficiently at cool, consistent temperatures, in clean water, with a steady feedstock supply. Lake Pend Oreille at Sandpoint, Idaho meets all three conditions with exceptional alignment: gravity-fed water at consistent cool temperatures year-round from the Clark Fork drainage, the Rathdrum Prairie Aquifer supplying without petroleum pumping, and the Palouse canola corridor eighty miles south delivering glycerin by rail with every ecodiesel season. A PHA fermentation cooperative at Sandpoint converts the canola press's waste stream into the cold-climate food forest's primary greenhouse covering — the most direct closed loop in the entire program.

PHA copolymer blending addresses the cold-climate brittleness concern directly. Pure polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) becomes brittle at extreme cold; the copolymer PHBV — polyhydroxybutyrate-co-valerate — is less stiff, tougher, and maintains film integrity at temperatures relevant to Wyoming and northern Idaho winters. The Sandpoint fermentation cooperative produces PHBV specifically tuned for cold-climate performance rather than the simplest PHB formulation.

Beyond greenhouse film, PHA serves the full range of agricultural flexible film applications: row cover, mulch film, drip tape, seedling trays, and food packaging for the cooperative's harvest sales. The canola ecodiesel cooperative produces three PHA feedstreams from one crop: glycerin from the oil press, canola straw from the harvest residue, and press cake as soil amendment. The closed loop tightens with each additional feedstream.

### VI. Guayule Natural Rubber — The American Desert's Sovereign Elastic

*Parthenium argentatum* — guayule — is a perennial shrub native to the Chihuahuan Desert of southern New Mexico, Arizona, and west Texas. It grows on the same dry slopes and arroyos where the acequia gravity-fed irrigation channels were carved by the ancestors of the communities still living there. It is not a crop displaced into an unsuitable climate. It is a sovereign American plant, growing in sovereign American soil, producing natural rubber latex without a single container ship departing from Southeast Asia.

Guayule latex is hypoallergenic — containing none of the Hevea tree proteins responsible for latex allergies in conventional tropical rubber. New Mexico State University and the University of Arizona carry active guayule latex extraction programs. The USDA Agricultural Research Service has documented cultivation protocols across the Chihuahuan and Sonoran Desert regions. The science is deployment-ready.

For cold-climate greenhouse infrastructure specifically, guayule rubber serves as the structural sealing system: rubber gaskets sealing polycarbonate or PHA film panels to frame members, rubber edge strips flexing with thermal expansion rather than cracking, rubber weather stripping on greenhouse doors and vents, and heavy rubber-coated canvas tarps for overnight frost protection, root cellar covers, and light-deprivation structures. Every one of these applications currently depends on petroleum-derived synthetic rubber. A domestic guayule cooperative in the Rio Grande watershed supplies them all.

The Rio Grande Cooperative operates within a full Helen Atthowe-style veganic food forest polyculture. The canopy layer belongs to native nitrogen-fixing trees — desert ironwood, velvet mesquite, and blue palo verde — whose shade creates the cooler microclimate that guayule favors for peak latex accumulation. Guayule occupies the mid-story on a staggered three-to-five-year harvest rotation. The ground layer is a native legume guild — tepary bean, desert marigold, jojoba — holding the soil surface and feeding the mycorrhizal network. The food harvest includes saguaro fruit, pomegranate, fig, jujube, and moringa. The rubber cooperative and the food forest are one system.

The Rio Grande Cooperative ships guayule biomass north by rail to a processing hub in Whitefish, Montana, where latex is extracted, vulcanized, and distributed to food forest cooperatives across the corridor. The press cake returns south by rail as compost amendment. The aquifer discipline is absolute: the cooperative draws no more water than the monsoon reliably recharges, tracked in real time through the Grain OS materials passport.

### VII. Hemp Composites — The Cold-Climate Insulated Structure

Industrial hemp is a carbon-capturing, soil-regenerating crop that requires minimal pesticides, minimal synthetic fertilizer, little water to farm relative to most grain crops, and sequesters approximately nine tons of CO2 per acre during its growing season. Its fibers are longer and in many applications stronger than the wood fibers found in standard structural lumber. Its woody inner core — called hurd or shiv — combines with lime and water to produce hempcrete, a biocomposite insulation and walling material with a thermal conductivity of 0.06 to 0.6 W/m·K, a total porosity of 68 to 80 percent, and moisture-regulating properties that allow walls to absorb humidity in surplus and release it in deficit — eliminating the condensation and mold cycles that plague conventional cold-climate construction.

The structural deployment milestone came in December 2024, when Sativa Building Systems received ICC-ES evaluation for the Z Panel — the first precast hempcrete product in the United States to demonstrate compliance with International Residential Code and International Building Code requirements in all 50 states. A greenhouse structure built from Z Panels in Wisconsin in 2018 has survived five Wisconsin winters and a tornado. Hempcrete greenhouse construction is code-compliant, cold-tested, and commercially available today.

For the cold-climate food forest season-extension program, hempcrete Z Panels serve as the insulated end walls of hoop houses and high tunnel greenhouses, the walls of seed storage buildings, propagation houses, worker break rooms, and tool sheds, and the foundation for permanent food forest shelter structures. A 12-inch hempcrete wall delivers an R-value of approximately R-28, with thermal mass that further reduces heating energy requirements beyond what the raw R-value suggests. The result is a growing structure that stays warm through a Sheridan County, Wyoming winter using minimal supplemental heat — extending the food forest's productive season by six to eight weeks on each end.

Hemp wool batt insulation, produced from the bast fiber separated during hemp decortication, delivers an R-value of approximately R-3.7 per inch — comparable to fiberglass and rock wool — with zero itch, zero off-gassing, zero toxic binders, and full compostability at end of life.

The bottleneck in domestic hemp building material production is decortication infrastructure — the mechanical separation of bast fiber from hurd core. Each watershed node that establishes a decortication cooperative unlocks both the insulation stream and the hempcrete stream simultaneously. The Grain OS program funds Job Guarantee workers to build and operate these facilities in each watershed node, with processed panels and batts moving by rail to neighboring nodes that have demand exceeding their own growing capacity.

The proximity principle governs hemp logistics: grow hemp as close as possible to the decortication facility, and ship finished building products by rail rather than raw biomass. This is the same logic as pressing canola into ecodiesel at the cooperative before shipping the fuel rather than shipping raw seed. Value concentration at the point of production, distribution by rail, sovereignty at the watershed level.

## Part Three: The Season-Extension Architecture

### VIII. The Cold-Climate Hoop House as Integrated Materials System

A food forest season-extension hoop house in the Spokane, Sandpoint, Whitefish, or Sheridan corridor uses every material stream in this proposal simultaneously, and the assembly tells the story of the whole system in one structure.

The glazing layer is PHBV copolymer film — cold-flexible, compostable, produced at the Sandpoint fermentation cooperative from canola glycerin arriving by rail from the Palouse. The film transmits the full photosynthetically active radiation spectrum plants require, provides infrared thermal retention for cold nights, and composts into the garden soil at the end of its service life with no microplastic residue.

The structural hoops are galvanized steel — the most durable and recyclable structural option, sourced from domestic steel recycling streams and tracked through the Grain OS materials passport. The frame connections use guayule rubber gaskets and edge strips, sealing PHA film to steel without cracking under thermal cycling, sourced from the Rio Grande Cooperative via BNSF rail to the Whitefish distribution hub.

The insulated end walls are hempcrete Z Panels — code-compliant, moisture-regulating, carbon-sequestering throughout their service life — with hemp wool batt insulation in any framed wall sections. Grown on the Palouse or in Sheridan County, decorticated at the watershed cooperative, panelized at the regional manufacturing facility, delivered by rail.

The overnight frost protection tarp is guayule rubber-coated canvas — durable, flexible at -40°F, locally stored, and reused for a decade before recycling. The drip irrigation tape is PHA film — compostable in the soil at end of service, leaving no plastic residue in the growing bed.

The entire structure is petroleum-free in its material composition. Every component is traceable to its origin watershed through the Grain OS materials passport. Every component either composts, recycles, or sequesters carbon across its service life. The food forest grows beneath it, extending its productive season by six to eight weeks on each end, feeding the cooperative workers who built the structure and the neighbors who depend on what grows inside it.

### IX. The Helen Atthowe Integration

Helen Atthowe's veganic permaculture framework — developed across decades of research at Woodleaf Farm in Montana, Oregon, and California — provides the growing design philosophy that the season-extension infrastructure serves. No-till soil management. Polyculture guild planting. Insectary strips attracting beneficial predators and pollinators. Living mulch systems holding moisture, building organic matter, and suppressing competition simultaneously. Mycorrhizal network cultivation that turns the soil into a communication and nutrient-sharing system operating far below the surface.

The Grain OS adaptation of the Atthowe framework for cold-climate nodes pairs the outdoor perennial food forest with the season-extension hoop house as two phases of one system. The outdoor food forest — hazelnut alleys, apple and pear corridors, elderberry hedgerows, nitrogen-fixing understory — produces the multi-decade compounding harvest that is the program's long-term wealth engine. The season-extension hoop house produces greens, root crops, herbs, and early starts for the outdoor planting — extending the productive window and providing continuous fresh food for cooperative workers and community members through the shoulder seasons and the deep winter.

The fertility is free. The water arrives by snowmelt and gravity. The fuel comes from canola pressed on-site. The greenhouse covering composts back into the soil. The insulated walls sequester carbon across their service life. The rubber seals flex and hold through the coldest nights the watershed produces. The system nourishes, it circulates, and it returns what it takes.

## Part Four: The Geographic Nodes

### X. The Five Watershed Nodes

**Flathead Valley, Montana — The Northern Processing Hub**

The BNSF Empire Builder stops at Whitefish daily. The Flathead River delivers glacier-fed water from the Cabinet and Swan Ranges. Food forest agriculture, CSA farming, and community grain growing are already organized and active. Canola ecodiesel from the Palouse corridor travels north by rail. Whitefish serves as the processing hub for guayule biomass arriving by rail from the Rio Grande Cooperative — latex extraction, vulcanization, and distribution to the cold-climate nodes of the corridor. Hemp grown in the Flathead Valley feeds a decortication cooperative producing hempcrete panels and hemp wool batts for the regional construction market. Montana's absence of sales tax on most goods and its 0.50 percent average property tax rate support cooperative land ownership.

**Lake Pend Oreille, Idaho — The Fermentation and Film Node**

Sandpoint's gravity-fed drinking water from Lake Pend Oreille, the Clark Fork drainage's consistent cool temperatures, and the Rathdrum Prairie Aquifer's abundance without petroleum pumping make this the strongest single site in the constellation for PHA fermentation. Canola glycerin arrives by rail from the Palouse eighty miles south. PHBV copolymer film, compostable drip tape, agricultural row cover, and food packaging film ship outward by the same Empire Builder rail connection. The Sandpoint node is the materials manufacturing heart of the northern Idaho-Montana corridor.

**Champlain Basin, Vermont and New York — The Eastern Anchor**

The Amtrak Vermonter runs daily to New York City. Vermont's food cooperative infrastructure is the most developed in New England. Two trillion gallons of annual inflow from the Green Mountains and Adirondacks. Vermont's sugar maple and sugar beet industries produce fermentable sugars at cooperative scale — a PHA feedstock stream fully independent of the canola glycerin supply, making the Champlain node the most feedstock-resilient in the network. Hemp growing and natural building practice are further advanced in New England than in any other region of the country, with decortication infrastructure actively developing. The Champlain node supplies the northeast urban corridor with food forest produce, hemp building materials, and compostable packaging film by rail.

**Sheridan County, Wyoming — The Plains Agricultural Node**

BNSF rail connects Sheridan north to Billings and south through the Powder River Basin network. The Big Horn Mountains deliver snowmelt recharge to the alluvial aquifers of Sheridan County. Rooted in Wyoming, the county's food sovereignty organization, received state food security funding in 2025. Land cost in Sheridan County is substantially lower than any Pacific Northwest equivalent, making it the most accessible node for cooperative land acquisition. Wyoming has zero state individual income tax and no corporate income tax, maximizing the financial return to cooperative workers and investors. The growing season at 3,700 feet elevation runs 90 to 110 days — real and productive for food forest establishment, hemp cultivation, and canola ecodiesel growing. Sheridan becomes the plains anchor of the corridor, supplying the Spokane processing hub with raw canola and hemp, and receiving processed ecodiesel, greenhouse film, and building panels in return.

**Rio Grande Cooperative, New Mexico and Arizona — The Desert Rubber Node**

The summer monsoon delivers 7 to 12 inches of precipitation annually across the Chihuahuan and Sonoran Desert regions, recharging aquifers whose geology has accumulated water across centuries of patient hydrology. The acequia gravity-fed irrigation tradition provides the water-sharing governance model. BNSF and Union Pacific both traverse this corridor. New Mexico State University and the University of Arizona carry active guayule latex research programs. The Tohono O'odham Nation's seed-saving and water-harvesting knowledge, developed across centuries of desert agriculture in the heart of guayule country, is honored as a foundational source of the program's desert farming wisdom. The Rio Grande Cooperative ships processed guayule biomass north to Whitefish by rail, receives press cake compost amendment in return, and operates a full food forest polyculture alongside the rubber guild — feeding its workers and its neighbors from the same landscape that produces the domestic rubber supply.

## Part Five: The Funding Architecture

### XI. Modern Monetary Theory as the Investment Framework

The federal government of the United States is the sole issuer of the U.S. dollar. It does not fund its spending the way a household funds its spending — by earning income first and spending from that income. The government of a sovereign currency-issuing nation creates money when it spends and destroys money when it taxes. This is not a controversial claim; it is an accurate description of how sovereign fiat currency actually works, confirmed by the operational practices of the Federal Reserve and the Treasury documented in publicly available accounting records.

The real constraints on federal investment are labor, materials, and ecological capacity — not money. When the federal government invests in a program that employs willing workers to produce real goods from renewable domestic materials, it is not taking money from somewhere else. It is directing the economy's real productive capacity toward a purpose the market undervalues because the market does not price the full cost of petroleum dependency, ecological degradation, and community economic erosion.

This program employs willing workers. It produces real goods — food, fuel, rubber, bioplastic, insulation, greenhouse structures. It builds from renewable domestic materials. It operates within the ecological discipline of watershed-scale stewardship. Every dollar the federal government issues to fund this program sets workers doing work the country needs, producing materials the country needs, from soil and water and plant biology the country already holds.

### XII. The Job Guarantee as the Program's Spine

The Job Guarantee is the federal commitment to employ every willing and able worker at a living wage, providing a price anchor for the economy, a permanent floor beneath poverty, and a continuous public investment in the productive capacity of American communities.

Every canola farm in the Palouse and the Flathead Valley is a Job Guarantee site. Every guayule food forest guild in the Rio Grande watershed is a Job Guarantee site. Every PHA fermentation facility at Sandpoint, every hemp decortication cooperative in Vermont, every hoop house construction crew in Sheridan County is a Job Guarantee site. The work is real. The wages are dignified. The products nourish the communities that produce them.

The Job Guarantee does not compete with private employment. It provides a wage floor that private employers must meet or exceed to attract workers. It employs people in the places where private investment has historically underinvested — rural watersheds, high-elevation agricultural communities, desert cooperative farming regions — and it produces the infrastructure that makes those places more economically vibrant for every subsequent private investment.

### XIII. The Materials Passport and the Transparent Ledger

Every manufactured object in this program carries a Grain OS materials passport — a digital record of its material composition, its origin watershed, its production method, and its intended end-of-life pathway. The PHA greenhouse film knows which canola field produced its glycerin and which composting facility will receive it at end of service. The hempcrete Z Panel knows which Wyoming field grew its hemp and what its carbon sequestration balance is across its service life. The guayule rubber gasket knows which Rio Grande guild produced its latex and which recycling stream handles it at end of product life.

This transparency serves investors, farmers, customers, neighbors, and the federal agencies responsible for verifying that public investment produces the ecological and community outcomes it promises. The Grain OS ledger is open-source software published under CC BY-SA 4.0 and auditable by any party with an interest in the program's outcomes.

### XIV. The County Currency Integration

Spokane County's SPOK currency — a proposed county-sanctioned, tax-receivable digital instrument denominated at dollar parity and running on the Grain OS ledger — circulates the value of the food forest, ecodiesel, and building materials economy within the county rather than draining it to distant financial centers. SPOK joins a constellation of watershed currencies — RIO in the Rio Grande Cooperative, and future instruments in the Flathead Valley and Champlain Basin nodes — all interoperable through the Solana settlement layer at transparent, market-governed exchange rates.

County currencies are constitutional as cooperative exchange instruments and tax-receivable vouchers rather than legal tender. The legal architecture is careful, documented, and built with the counsel of constitutional scholars. The experiment is worth conducting because the current monetary system concentrates wealth in financial centers and extracts it from agricultural regions, and a locally circulating instrument returns that wealth to the communities that produced it.

## Part Six: The Legislative Ask

### XV. Five Program Authorizations

**Authorization One: The Sovereign Canola Ecodiesel Corridor.** Federal Job Guarantee funding for cooperative canola cultivation, cold-press ecodiesel production, and transesterification facilities across the Palouse, Flathead Valley, Big Horn Basin, and Champlain Basin nodes. Non-GMO, open-pollinated seed libraries funded through the USDA Agricultural Research Service. Cooperative ownership structures eligible for USDA cooperative development grants. Target: 500,000 acres of cooperative canola cultivation within five years, producing 65 to 80 million gallons of ecodiesel annually for rail corridor and agricultural fuel use.

**Authorization Two: The PHA Bioplastic Fermentation Program.** Federal investment in PHA fermentation cooperative infrastructure at Sandpoint, Idaho and Burlington, Vermont as anchor nodes, with secondary facilities funded in each watershed node as canola glycerin and lignocellulosic biomass supply chains mature. Product mandate: PHBV copolymer greenhouse film, agricultural row cover, compostable food packaging, and drip irrigation tape as primary outputs. End-of-life composting infrastructure funded alongside production infrastructure. Target: domestic PHA production sufficient to replace 25 percent of agricultural polyethylene film use within seven years.

**Authorization Three: The Domestic Guayule Rubber Program.** USDA and NRCS cooperative agreements with Rio Grande watershed acequia associations and tribal sovereignty partners for guayule cultivation and food forest polyculture establishment. Processing hub infrastructure funded at Whitefish, Montana for latex extraction and vulcanization. Rail freight agreements with BNSF for biomass movement. End-market development for guayule rubber in agricultural sealing components, cold-climate protective tarps, and domestic medical supply chain applications. Target: 50,000 acres of guayule cultivation within five years, sufficient to supply domestic agricultural rubber demand.

**Authorization Four: The Hemp Building Materials Program.** Job Guarantee funding for hemp cultivation and decortication cooperative infrastructure in each watershed node. ICC-ES code-compliant hempcrete panel and hemp wool batt production facilities. Building code education and contractor training programs ensuring that every region served by this program has trained builders capable of working with hemp composite materials. Target: domestic hemp building material production sufficient to insulate and wall 10,000 food forest cooperative structures within five years.

**Authorization Five: The Grain OS Materials Passport and Transparent Ledger.** Federal open-source software infrastructure investment for the Grain OS ledger, materials passport system, and county currency settlement layer. Published under CC BY-SA 4.0. Available to every cooperative, county government, and federal agency in the program at no license cost. Operated as public infrastructure analogous to the interstate highway system — funded publicly, available to all, governed transparently.

## Part Seven: The Silken Ground Rubric

### XVI. Does It Nourish?

The canola food forest polyculture feeds the cooperative workers who tend it, the neighbors who buy at the market, and the rail workers who carry what the land grows. The guayule desert food forest feeds the Rio Grande communities who have farmed this landscape for centuries. The cold-climate hoop house extends the growing season by six to eight weeks on each end, producing greens and root crops and herbs through the shoulder seasons when outdoor harvests are sparse. The hempcrete shelter building stores seeds, tools, and knowledge. The PHA film protects the seedlings through the last frost and the first freeze. The program nourishes.

### XVII. Does It Circulate?

The canola glycerin flows from the ecodiesel press to the fermentation tank. The PHA film flows from the fermentation cooperative to the food forest hoop house. The guayule biomass flows north by rail to the processing hub. The press cake flows south by rail as compost amendment. The SPOK and RIO currencies flow through cooperative payrolls, farmers markets, county tax receipts, and seasonal reissuance. The rail connects every node to every other node. The program circulates.

### XVIII. Does It Return What It Takes?

The guayule root system stabilizes desert soil. The food forest guild builds organic matter year over year across every watershed. The hemp crop sequesters nine tons of CO2 per acre during its growing season and continues sequestering carbon in the hempcrete walls for the lifetime of the building. The PHA film composts into the soil that grew the canola that produced the glycerin that fed the bacteria that extruded the film. The aquifer draw in the Rio Grande node stays below the monsoon recharge rate, tracked in real time through the materials passport. The program returns what it takes.

## Conclusion: The Work Is Ready

The plants are already growing in American soil. The rail lines are already running through American watersheds. The research programs are already operating at New Mexico State, the University of Arizona, WSU Pullman, University of Vermont, and the USDA Agricultural Research Service. The cooperative organizations are already present in every watershed node this program proposes to serve. The building code compliance for hempcrete is established. The PHA greenhouse film is recognized in the horticultural literature. The guayule latex extraction protocols are documented and deployment-ready.

What this program provides is the federal investment to connect what already exists — the plants, the rail, the research, the cooperative organizations, the building codes — into a sovereign domestic materials economy that feeds American families, fuels American rail, covers American food forests with compostable film, insulates American cooperative buildings with carbon-sequestering hemp, and seals American greenhouse structures with domestic desert rubber.

The work is real. The workers are ready. The land is patient and deep, and it has always been ready.

We ask Congress to begin.
