Something beautiful is growing in the American watershed.

Three river valleys and one desert basin — one in northwestern Montana, one in northern Idaho, one stretching across Vermont and New York, one fed by the summer monsoons of the American Southwest — hold some of the cleanest, most renewable freshwater on this continent. Snowmelt arrives by gravity every spring, unrequested, filling lakes that have never needed a pump to fill. Summer monsoons recharge ancient aquifers that have held water across centuries of patient geology. Rail lines already connect these places to one another and to the communities that need what they grow. The soil here is patient and deep, and it is ready.

We are planting food forests in these valleys. We are pressing canola oil for clean fuel. We are growing rubber in the desert from a plant that has always lived there. We are building cooperatives that pay fair wages and keep the value of the harvest circulating inside the communities that grow it. We are inviting a small group of investors to help us do this well and do this together.

This letter is that invitation.

## How We Got Here

For the past few decades, the United States imported a great deal of its energy, a great deal of its food, a great deal of its manufactured goods, and nearly all of its natural rubber from places far away, through supply chains that worked smoothly as long as oil stayed inexpensive and the world stayed peaceful. Cheap oil made it possible to grow food with petroleum-based fertilizers, ship it thousands of miles, and sell it at prices that made local farming difficult to sustain. Cheap oil made it possible to build appliances and packaging from petroleum-based plastics that entered our kitchens and, gradually, our bodies. Cheap oil made it possible to run animal agriculture at a scale and speed that no traditional farming community would have recognized as farming. And cheap petrochemical feedstocks displaced the domestic rubber agriculture this country once knew how to cultivate.

We are a nation that learned, slowly and sometimes painfully, that the things we accepted as cheap often carried costs that showed up later — in our watersheds, in our hospitals, in the communities downstream from the facilities that produced what we consumed. The people who bore the most of that cost were often the people who benefited the least from the arrangement.

This is a hard story to tell gently, so we will tell it the way Dr. K'adamawe K'nife tells it in Jamaica when he talks about food crime: with love for the people who were hurt, with respect for how hard they worked within the system they were given, and with the firm belief that people change course when they see a better path clearly.

The better path is already here. We are walking it.

## What Changed This Spring

In late February 2026, a military conflict in the Middle East disrupted the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil supply normally flows. Oil prices rose sharply. Fertilizer prices, which depend on petroleum-based production, are projected to double before the planting season ends. Supply chains that once seemed permanent now feel more fragile than anyone planned for.

This moment is uncomfortable. It is also clarifying.

Every farm that grows food through living soil — through biological nitrogen fixation, through perennial root systems, through the patient relationship between plants and fungi and microorganisms — grows just as productively this spring as it did last spring. Every food forest that draws its water from snowmelt and its fertility from composted plant matter and its fuel from canola pressed on-site stands on the same ground it stood on before the price of oil changed. The disruption revealed what was already true: the food systems that work with nature cost less to maintain, and they hold steady when distant supply chains do not.

This is a signal worth paying attention to. The clarity it brings is a gift.

## The Three Valleys

**The Flathead Valley in Montana** receives its water from the glaciers of the Cabinet and Swan Ranges. The Flathead River runs clean and cold through a valley where food forest agriculture, CSA farming, and community grain growing are already organized and active. The BNSF Empire Builder stops at Whitefish station every morning. Canola ecodiesel from the Palouse corridor, eighty miles southeast, travels north by rail and costs less per gallon to press than conventional diesel costs to purchase. Whitefish serves as the northern processing hub for the entire corridor — the place where guayule biomass arriving from the Southwest is extracted, vulcanized, and prepared for domestic rubber manufacturing, powered by the same ecodiesel the valley grows.

**Lake Pend Oreille in Idaho** is the fifth-deepest lake in the nation — 44 million acre-feet of glacially-carved freshwater, fed by the Clark Fork River's entire western Montana drainage. Sandpoint draws its drinking water from the lake by gravity. The Empire Builder stops here too. The Spokane Valley-Rathdrum Prairie Aquifer beneath the valley supplies half a million people without a single petroleum-powered pumping station. The cool, consistent temperatures of the Clark Fork drainage make Sandpoint the strongest single site in the constellation for PHA fermentation — converting canola glycerin byproduct from the Palouse into compostable bioplastic film, with zero-pump water and daily rail connection to every market the cooperative serves.

**The Champlain Basin in Vermont and New York** receives two trillion gallons of inflow each year from the Green Mountains and the Adirondacks. Vermont is already the most locally food-sovereign state in New England. Burlington's food co-op, farmers markets, and CSA networks have been building community food sovereignty for decades. The Amtrak Vermonter runs south to New York City. Vermont's sugar maple and sugar beet industries produce fermentable sugars at cooperative scale — a second PHA fermentation feedstock stream entirely independent of the Palouse glycerin corridor, making the Champlain node the most feedstock-resilient site in the network. The lake itself, at 120 miles long, is one of the great freshwater resources of the eastern United States, and the watershed communities that tend it are already organized and ready to grow more.

## The Desert Basin: Rio Grande Cooperative

There is a fourth geography that belongs in this vision. It does not hold snowmelt. It holds something older and quieter: the monsoon.

Across southern New Mexico, Arizona, and the high desert of west Texas, the summer monsoon arrives every July with reliable generosity. Seven to twelve inches of summer precipitation recharge underground aquifers that have been accumulating water through centuries of patient geology. The acequias of New Mexico — gravity-fed irrigation channels whose design predates any European presence on this continent by centuries — demonstrate how desert communities share water with equity and care. The land here is dry on the surface and rich beneath it, which is precisely why it has sustained human civilization for so long.

This is guayule country.

*Parthenium argentatum* — guayule — is a perennial shrub native to the Chihuahuan Desert. It grows on the same dry slopes and arroyos where the acequias were carved. A cooperative draws no more water than the monsoon reliably recharges — a discipline the acequia tradition has honored for centuries and that the Grain OS materials passport makes transparent for every investor and every neighbor. New Mexico State University and the University of Arizona both carry active guayule latex research programs. BNSF and Union Pacific both traverse this corridor. The science is ready. The land is ready. The rail lines are already there.

Guayule latex is hypoallergenic — containing none of the proteins responsible for latex allergies in conventional tropical rubber — making it suitable for medical supply chains, barrier manufacturing, and industrial applications that conventional synthetic rubber serves today with petroleum feedstocks. A domestic guayule cooperative replaces all of that with a plant that belongs to this land, grown by workers who belong to this community, shipped north by rail to the Flathead Valley processing hub, and tracked at every step through the Grain OS materials passport.

We call this node the Rio Grande Cooperative, denominated in the RIO currency — dollar-parity, tax-receivable, and interoperable through the Solana settlement layer with SPOK in Spokane County and the other currencies in the Grain OS constellation.

## The Helen Atthowe Integration: Food Forest Polyculture in the Desert

Helen Atthowe's ecological farm synthesis — developed across decades at Woodleaf Farm in Montana and Eastern Oregon — is built on principles that translate directly into desert agroforestry. No-till soil management. Polyculture guild planting. Insectary strips that attract beneficial predators and pollinators. Living mulch systems that hold moisture, suppress competing growth, and build organic matter simultaneously. Mycorrhizal network cultivation that turns the soil into a communication and nutrient-sharing system operating far below the surface.

In a desert context, these principles do not weaken — they strengthen. The desert soil is not poor. It is patient. It holds mineral richness that humid-climate soils have long since leached away. What it requires is organic matter to hold the moisture that rainfall delivers, and the right companion plantings to create the microclimate that allows moisture to be used slowly and completely rather than evaporated in the first afternoon of summer sun.

The Rio Grande food forest guild builds this microclimate in three layers. The canopy belongs to native nitrogen-fixing trees: desert ironwood, velvet mesquite, and blue palo verde — all Sonoran natives whose canopy creates dappled shade that reduces soil temperature, conserves moisture, and produces the cooler microclimate that guayule favors for peak latex accumulation. Guayule occupies the mid-story: a perennial that does not need replanting each season, harvested on a staggered three-to-five-year rotation so the cooperative always holds mature stands in production. The ground layer is a native legume guild — tepary bean, desert marigold, and jojoba — holding the soil surface and feeding the mycorrhizal network that connects all the roots in the guild.

The food harvest from this polyculture is genuinely generous. Saguaro and organ pipe cacti, managed cooperatively rather than harvested destructively, produce fruit in June — a traditional food of the Tohono O'odham Nation, whose territory overlaps with the heart of guayule country and whose centuries of seed-saving and water-harvesting knowledge are among the most refined desert agriculture wisdom on this continent. Pomegranate and fig thrive in the same elevation band where guayule grows. Jujube produces prolifically in desert heat with minimal water. Moringa — whose protein-rich leaves and oil-rich seeds make it one of the most nutritionally complete trees in the world — grows vigorously in the same climate zone.

This is not a monoculture rubber farm with food as an afterthought. This is a food forest in which guayule is one productive layer among many — the layer whose raw biomass ships north by rail for processing while the other layers feed the cooperative workers, seed the local market, and compost back into the soil that holds the whole guild together.

The guayule press cake after latex extraction returns south by rail as compost amendment for the desert food forest. The soil that grew the rubber receives the rubber back as fertility. The domestic mine is the recycling stream. Nothing that left the watershed fails to return.

## Aquifer Sovereignty and Monsoon Stewardship

The Southwest's underground water commons is a geological inheritance deserving the same reverence as the snowmelt-fed lakes of Montana and Idaho. The Grain OS discipline is simple: the cooperative draws no more than the monsoon reliably recharges. In a good monsoon year, the aquifer recharge rate is calculable and the cooperative's draw rate is set below it. In a dry year, the cooperative reduces its draw accordingly, allowing the guayule — a plant whose entire evolutionary heritage is surviving extended drought — to demonstrate exactly what it was designed to do.

Drip irrigation from gravity-fed tanks elevated above the field eliminates pump energy wherever topography allows. The food forest's deep root systems and living mulch cover reduce evaporation so dramatically that water demand per acre of managed polyculture is a fraction of what conventional annual-crop agriculture requires on the same land.

The Grain OS materials passport tracks water use per kilogram of guayule biomass produced and per pound of food harvested. Every stakeholder — investor, farmer, customer, neighbor — can see exactly how much water the cooperative drew, how much the monsoon recharged, and what the net aquifer balance is for the season. The aquifer is the commons. The commons belongs to everyone who depends on it, including the people not yet born.

## What We Are Building

In each of these nodes, we are establishing veganic food forest cooperatives — perennial growing systems that produce fruit, nuts, legumes, oil crops, medicinal herbs, and raw industrial biomaterials without synthetic fertilizers, without chemical pesticides, and without animal inputs of any kind. These systems build soil fertility through the work of the plants themselves: nitrogen-fixing legumes in the understory, deep-rooted perennials that bring minerals up from bedrock, chop-and-drop mulching that feeds the soil surface each season.

The fertility is free. The water arrives by snowmelt, rain, and managed aquifer draw below recharge. The fuel comes from canola pressed on-site. The rubber grows in the desert and ships north by rail. The bioplastic ferments from glycerin at the edge of the coldest gravity-fed lake in the inland Northwest. The distribution moves by rail. The economic value stays inside the watershed through the SPOK county currency in Spokane County and the RIO currency in the Rio Grande Cooperative — tax-receivable, dollar-parity instruments that keep cooperative revenue circulating locally rather than draining to distant shareholders.

The cooperatives pay living wages to the people who tend them. They offer fair-trade pricing to the communities they serve. They make their accounting transparent through the Grain OS ledger so every stakeholder — investor, farmer, customer, neighbor — can see exactly where every dollar flows.

## The Sixth Layer: PHA Fermentation at Sandpoint

Canola ecodiesel pressing produces glycerin as a byproduct. In a conventional ecodiesel facility, glycerin is a waste stream. In the Grain OS cooperative model, glycerin is a feedstock.

Polyhydroxyalkanoate — PHA — is a bioplastic produced by bacteria that consume fermentable sugars and excrete a flexible, compostable polymer as their metabolic byproduct. Fermentation runs best at cool, consistent temperatures, in clean water, with a steady feedstock supply. Lake Pend Oreille at Sandpoint meets all three conditions with exceptional elegance: gravity-fed water at consistent cool temperatures year-round, the aquifer supplying without petroleum pumping, and the Palouse canola corridor eighty miles south delivering glycerin by rail with every ecodiesel season.

A PHA fermentation cooperative at Sandpoint converts what would otherwise be a disposal problem into compostable flexible packaging, barrier films, and next-generation non-latex barrier products — all manufactured domestically, all compostable at end of life, all traceable through the Grain OS materials passport from the canola field where the glycerin originated to the composting facility where the product returns to soil.

The guayule rubber cooperative and the PHA fermentation cooperative are complementary in their material properties and their market reach. Together they cover the full range of petroleum-derived polymer applications — elastic barrier materials and flexible film — replacing both with domestic, plant-derived, fully compostable materials tracked at every step through a transparent open ledger.

## The Return We Offer

The hazelnut produces its first meaningful harvest in year four. The apple reaches full yield in year seven. The walnut canopy closes in year twelve. The guayule stand reaches peak latex yield in year three and is harvested on a staggered rotation that keeps the cooperative in continuous production. The food forest is a vehicle for durable income — from the harvest itself, from canola ecodiesel sales to the rail corridor cooperative, from guayule latex sales to domestic rubber manufacturing, from PHA fermentation film sales to food packaging cooperatives, from fair-trade processing and distribution through the Grain OS platform, and from the land appreciation that follows when a watershed's food security and materials sovereignty are demonstrably improved.

The Grain OS ledger makes every one of these revenue streams transparent, auditable, and reported to investors in plain language. The cooperative structure ensures that investors share in the surplus after the people who tend the land are paid first. This is the fair-trade principle applied to investment structure: the people closest to the work are the first to benefit from it.

## Who This Is For

This invitation goes to investors who share a recognition that has been building across very different communities for a long time now.

It goes to the vegan investor who has spent years funding plant-based products and wants to ensure those products have a food-secure, petroleum-resilient supply chain behind them — one that includes the packaging and the rubber materials, not only the food itself.

It goes to the conservative investor who believes in local food sovereignty, sound land stewardship, community self-reliance, and the principle that a community able to feed and supply itself from its own watershed and desert is a community that stands on ground no distant disruption can take from it.

It goes to the impact investor who understands that the communities most harmed by the disrupted supply chain — rural workers, downstream neighbors, desert communities whose water has been overpumped for extraction — deserve supply chains designed for their wellbeing rather than for maximum extraction.

It goes to anyone who has ever grown a garden and understood, in the quiet way that gardens teach, that patience and care produce abundance that no quarterly earnings report can fully describe.

## A Simple Invitation

We tend good land in good watersheds. We grow food that nourishes people without harming animals. We press fuel that runs clean and keeps value inside the communities that press it. We grow rubber in the desert from a plant that belongs to this land. We ferment glycerin into bioplastic at the edges of the coldest, cleanest, gravity-fed lakes on the continent. We move goods by rail rather than by petroleum-dependent trucking. We pay fair wages and keep fair accounts.

The world is moving toward this way of doing things — some by choice, some by necessity, all of them arriving at the same recognition that Dr. K'nife offered to his community in Jamaica: the materials that sustain us have always been close by. We grew away from them for a while. We are growing back.

We would be glad to have you with us.
