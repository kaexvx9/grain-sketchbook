## Prologue: A Morning in the Willamette Valley

The fog lifts slowly over the Willamette Valley in late January, revealing fields that stretch to the horizon. From the highway, you see what looks like progress: vast operations, efficient systems, industrial agriculture at scale. But drive closer, and the story changes. The air carries a particular scent—not the rich earthiness of healthy soil, but something else, something chemical and heavy. The fields are too uniform, too quiet. The soil, when you examine it, feels wrong—compacted, lifeless, waiting.

I'm 29 years old, and I've spent the last several years thinking about what comes after this. Not just for myself, but for the land, for the communities that depend on it, for the future that's being shaped right now in places like this. I believe Oregon is uniquely positioned to lead something unprecedented—not just progressive policy, but a complete reimagining of how we live together, how we work, how we build, how we grow.

This isn't a utopian fantasy. It's a practical proposal that draws on principles that have worked for centuries, combined with technologies that are emerging right now. It's about taking what's broken and making it whole again. It's about taking what's extractive and making it regenerative. It's about taking what's controlled and making it free.

## Part One: The Architecture of Crisis

### The View from Above

Imagine flying over Oregon in a small plane. You'd see patterns that tell a story. In the Willamette Valley, the fields are geometric, precise, industrial. They follow the logic of efficiency, of scale, of profit. But efficiency at what cost? Scale for whose benefit? Profit for whom?

The housing crisis isn't separate from this. Drive through the suburbs, and you see the same logic: developments that prioritize profit over people, scale over community, efficiency over beauty. The houses are identical, the streets are wide, the trees are ornamental. Nothing is productive. Nothing is permanent. Everything is disposable.

The economic crisis isn't separate either. The same logic that creates factory farms creates precarious work. The same logic that creates suburban sprawl creates economic isolation. The same logic that extracts from the land extracts from people.

And the technology crisis? It's the same logic again. Control. Extraction. Profit. Your data, your devices, your digital life—all controlled by corporations that don't serve you, that serve shareholders, that serve growth for growth's sake.

These aren't separate problems. They're symptoms of a single system: one that prioritizes extraction over regeneration, control over freedom, profit over people.

### The View from Below

But there's another view. Walk through an old-growth forest in the Cascades, and you see a different logic. The trees are diverse, interdependent, resilient. They've been here for centuries. They'll be here for centuries more. They're productive—not just for timber, but for habitat, for carbon sequestration, for beauty, for meaning.

Or visit a traditional European town—one of those places that tourists photograph, that feel timeless. The buildings are made from local materials: stone from nearby quarries, timber from nearby forests, earth from the very ground they stand on. The streets are narrow, human-scaled. The walls define boundaries, create community. The trees are productive—fruit trees, nut trees, trees that provide fuel and food and beauty.

Or talk to a permaculture farmer who's been working the same piece of land for decades, building soil, creating habitat, growing food in ways that regenerate rather than deplete. The logic is different: work with the land, not against it. Build for the long term, not the short term. Create abundance, not scarcity.

These aren't nostalgic fantasies. They're proven approaches. They've worked for centuries. They can work now.

## Part Two: The Currency of Possibility

### How Money Actually Works

Modern Monetary Theory is often misunderstood. Critics say it's magic money, that it can't work, that it will cause hyperinflation. But here's what they miss: this is how money actually works. This is how it's always worked. The question isn't whether we can create currency—it's whether we have the political will to create it for public good rather than private profit.

Oregon is perfect for this experiment. We already have the right tax structure: no sales tax (which means working people keep more of their money) and progressive income tax (which means wealth pays more, not workers). This creates what economists call "effective demand"—when working people have money, they spend it, which creates jobs, which helps everyone.

Through an open-source currency system built on open-source infrastructure, Oregon can create a state currency—call it what you will—to fund a Job Guarantee program. Workers get paid $18 to $22 per hour, regionally adjusted. Benefits include healthcare, childcare, retirement. The program funds itself through currency creation, not taxation.

Now, there's an important constitutional consideration here. The U.S. Constitution prohibits states from coining money or emitting bills of credit. But here's what changes the equation: modern digital networking technology, combined with the federal government's ability to create dollars, means the federal sector can always outbid the private sector in any market for Oregon's currency. This isn't a threat to federal authority—it's a feature. The federal government maintains its position as the most trustworthy financial institution in the world, with unmatched stability in redeeming government bonds for ordinary living expenses. When state economies strengthen and liquidity improves, the value of the dollar rises—both domestically and in international markets. What works in Oregon can be replicated in other states, creating a competitive governance model that ultimately strengthens the dollar's position globally. Currency markets and exchanges don't weaken federal authority—they create healthy competition that makes the entire system more resilient.

This isn't theory. This is how the Federal Reserve creates money. This is how banks create money. The difference is that we'd be creating it for public good: for jobs, for housing, for agriculture, for communities.

### The Job Guarantee: Work That Matters

The Job Guarantee isn't make-work. It's work that matters: converting factory farms to permaculture food forests, building traditional urbanism housing communities, maintaining local technology infrastructure, teaching, creating, building.

Imagine a worker on a converted farm in the Willamette Valley. They're learning permaculture, building soil, planting food forests. They're working with the land, not against it. They're creating habitat, sequestering carbon, growing food. They're earning a living wage, building skills, contributing to something larger than themselves.

Or imagine a worker building a traditional urbanism community. They're learning timber framing, rammed earth construction, sustainable building techniques. They're working with local materials, building for permanence, creating beauty. They're part of a community, not isolated in a suburb.

Or imagine a worker maintaining local compute infrastructure. They're learning open-source technology, building skills in programming and systems administration. They're creating infrastructure that serves the community, not corporations. They're building something that lasts, that can be repaired, that puts power back in people's hands.

This is the Job Guarantee: work that matters, work that builds, work that regenerates.

## Part Three: The Conversion

### From Extraction to Regeneration

The proposal is concrete: Oregon uses currency-created dollars to purchase non-vegan animal factory farms and convert them to organic vegan permaculture food forests. This isn't about eliminating agriculture—it's about transforming it. From extraction to regeneration. From monoculture to diversity. From depletion to abundance.

The conversion process itself creates Job Guarantee work. Workers learn permaculture design, soil building, native plant integration, pollinator habitat creation. They work with principles that have been proven: no-till agriculture, cover crops, compost and biochar, working with the land rather than against it.

The results are measurable. Soil health improves. Water quality improves. Carbon is sequestered. Habitat is created. Food is grown. Communities are fed. The land is restored, not depleted.

### Regional Optimization: Oregon's Diverse Landscapes

Oregon isn't one place. It's many places, each with unique characteristics, opportunities, and challenges.

**The Willamette Valley** (Portland, Salem, Eugene): Here, the conversion targets are clear. Factory farms in the valley can become food forests: hemp, bamboo, timber, diverse fruit and nut trees. Higher density, urban agriculture integration. The climate is mild, the soil is rich, the potential is enormous.

**The High Desert** (Bend, Ashland, Rogue River, Klamath Falls): Here, the approach is different. Drought-resistant permaculture: agave, mesquite, native grains, sustainable timber. Lower density, larger agricultural buffers. Water conservation is critical. The landscape is different, but the principles are the same: work with the land, not against it.

**The Coast** (Astoria, Newport, Coos Bay): Marine permaculture integration, ocean resilience, tourism and agriculture hybrid economy. The opportunities are unique: kelp and seaweed farming, marine ecosystem restoration, coastal food forests with salt-tolerant plants, ocean-based carbon sequestration through kelp forests.

**The Columbia Gorge** (Hood River, The Dalles): Wind energy integration, existing fruit orchards, tourism economy. The transition is natural: from monoculture orchards to diverse food forests, from industrial agriculture to regenerative permaculture.

**Central Oregon** (Prineville, Redmond): High desert agriculture, renewable energy infrastructure, data center transition opportunities. The potential is there: sustainable agriculture, local technology infrastructure, community development.

Each region is different. Each requires a different approach. But the principles are the same: regeneration, not extraction. Abundance, not scarcity. Community, not isolation.

### The Technology Layer: Local Control, Global Connection

The technology infrastructure isn't separate from this vision. It's integrated. Local compute clusters, powered by sustainable energy (solar, wind, hydro), running open-source operating systems designed for repairability, privacy, and local control.

These clusters provide local internet infrastructure, educational platforms, community governance systems, local business networks, arts and culture platforms. They're not controlled by corporations. They're controlled by communities. They're not designed for profit. They're designed for service.

The farming technology is integrated too. Networked autonomous robotics and sensors, designed to be no-till compatible—lightweight, precise, respectful of soil health. They handle seeding, planting, weeding (precision, no herbicides), harvesting (selective, gentle), soil monitoring, pollinator support.

The robots are self-assemblable, open-source, modular. Workers learn to build them, maintain them, program them, optimize them. Technology creates jobs, not eliminates them. Technology serves communities, not corporations.

## Part Four: The Architecture of Community

### Traditional Urbanism: Not Nostalgia, Necessity

Traditional urbanism isn't about nostalgia. It's about building communities that are sustainable, beautiful, and human-scaled. It's about using materials that are local, renewable, and durable: wood, earth, brick, and stone instead of steel and glass.

**Walls**: They define community boundaries. They create sustainable limits—not just physical boundaries, but limits on population, resource use, and growth. They create a natural hierarchy: main streets versus alleys, gardens versus squares, orchards versus open fields. They're not barriers. They're definitions.

**Streets**: Narrow, human-scaled, mixed materials (stone, gravel, dirt, tarmac). Traffic calming through material variation. Mixed use: pedestrians, bicycles, occasional vehicles. The street isn't just infrastructure. It's community space.

**Water**: Canals and drains throughout neighborhoods. Bioswales and rain gardens. Aquatic plant cultivation in larger canals—watercress, lotus, water chestnuts, and other edible aquatic plants. Floating gardens and hydroponic systems. Graywater systems for irrigation. Water isn't just utility. It's part of the community's life, its beauty, its productivity.

**Trees**: Espaliered fruit trees on south-facing walls. Community orchards. Pollarded trees for fuel and feed. Market gardens within town walls. Native landscaping that supports healthy native insects and pollinators. Trees aren't just decoration. They're productivity, beauty, meaning.

**Buildings**: Multi-story, with ground-floor commercial spaces and residential above. Ground floors: sustainable retail, plant-based restaurants, art and music venues, coworking spaces, tea houses, poetry reading rooms. Upper floors: residential units (500 to 1,200 square feet) with private bathrooms, high-speed fiber internet, natural ventilation, passive solar design, balconies with espalier opportunities.

**Materials**: Timber frame construction with mass timber from sustainable forestry. Rammed earth walls and compressed earth blocks from local soil. Fired brick from local clay. Fieldstone and cut stone from local quarries. Everything is local, renewable, durable. Everything is permanent, not disposable.

### The Daily Rhythm

The community isn't just buildings. It's how people live together.

**Morning**: Education. Homeschooling, community classes, intergenerational learning. Academic subjects, technical skills, arts and creativity, physical health, emotional health, social health. Multifaceted, multicultural, spiritually enriching.

**Afternoon**: Job Guarantee work. Farming, construction, arts, services. Work that matters, work that builds, work that regenerates.

**Evening**: Community activities. Music, poetry, tea, coworking. Connection, creation, collaboration.

**Weekly**: Market days (local produce, crafts, services), community meetings (governance, planning), arts events (concerts, poetry readings, art shows), work parties (construction, farming, maintenance).

**Seasonal**: Planting and harvesting, construction (weather-dependent), arts and culture (indoor activities), community celebrations (harvest festivals, solstices).

This isn't utopia. This is how communities have worked for centuries. This is how they can work again.

## Part Five: The Implementation

### The Legislative Framework

This vision requires legislative action. Five bills, structured carefully, proposed strategically.

**Bill One: The Job Guarantee Act**. Establishes the Job Guarantee program. Creates state currency through open-source infrastructure. Funds the program through currency creation, not taxation. Sets wage rates ($18 to $22 per hour, regionally adjusted). Establishes benefits (healthcare, childcare, retirement).

**Bill Two: The Factory Farm Conversion Act**. Authorizes purchase of non-vegan animal factory farms. Establishes conversion to vegan permaculture food forests. Creates Job Guarantee work opportunities. Sets environmental standards (no-till, organic, native plants). Establishes regional optimization (West/East Cascades, coast, Columbia Gorge, central Oregon).

**Bill Three: The Traditional Urbanism Housing Act**. Authorizes traditional urbanism housing communities. Establishes sustainable materials requirements. Creates walkable, mixed-use districts. Sets density and design standards. Integrates with Job Guarantee program.

**Bill Four: The Technology Integration Act**. Establishes local compute cluster infrastructure. Creates sustainable energy requirements. Authorizes autonomous farming robotics. Sets data privacy and local control standards. Integrates with Job Guarantee program.

**Bill Five: The Education Innovation Act**. Authorizes open-source-inspired homeschooling programs. Establishes community education centers. Creates intergenerational learning opportunities. Sets multicultural and spiritual enrichment standards. Integrates with Job Guarantee program.
