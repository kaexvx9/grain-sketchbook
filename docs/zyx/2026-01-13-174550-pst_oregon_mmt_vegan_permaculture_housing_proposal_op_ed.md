## Introduction: Why Oregon, Why Now

I'm 29 years old, and I've spent years thinking about how we can build a better future. Not just for ourselves, but for our children, our communities, and the planet we all share. I believe Oregon is uniquely positioned to lead the way—not just in progressive policy, but in creating a model that other states can follow.

Here's my vision: Oregon uses Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) to create a Job Guarantee program that converts factory farms into vegan permaculture food forests, builds beautiful traditional urbanism housing communities, and integrates cutting-edge open-source technology that puts power back in people's hands. It's ambitious, yes. But it's also practical, necessary, and entirely possible.

Oregon already has the foundation: no sales tax (which helps working people), progressive income tax (which targets wealth, not workers), and a tradition of innovation and sustainability. What we need now is the vision to put these pieces together into something transformative.

## Part 1: The Problems We Face

Let me be clear about what we're up against. The challenges are real, and they're interconnected.

**Housing Crisis**: Working people can't afford homes. Rents are skyrocketing. Homelessness is growing. The traditional model of housing development—speculative, profit-driven, environmentally destructive—isn't working for most of us.

**Agriculture Crisis**: Factory farms are destroying our environment, causing immense animal suffering, and producing food that's neither healthy nor sustainable. The industrial agriculture model is extractive, not regenerative. It depletes soil, pollutes water, and contributes to climate change.

**Economic Crisis**: Too many people can't find work, or can't find work that pays a living wage. Economic inequality is growing. The gap between the wealthy and everyone else is widening. Working people are struggling, and the system isn't designed to help them.

**Technology Crisis**: Big tech companies control our data, our devices, and our digital lives. We can't repair our devices. We're tracked everywhere we go. Our privacy is violated daily. Technology should serve us, not the other way around.

These problems aren't separate. They're symptoms of a system that prioritizes profit over people, extraction over regeneration, control over freedom. But we can build something different. We can create a system that serves people, regenerates the environment, and empowers communities.

## Part 2: The Solution—An Integrated Vision

### Modern Monetary Theory: How Oregon Can Fund This

Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) is often misunderstood. Here's the simple truth: Oregon, as a state, can create its own currency. We don't need to tax working people to fund programs. We can create money to pay for public goods—like a Job Guarantee program that ensures everyone who wants work can get it.

Oregon is perfect for MMT because we already have the right tax structure: no sales tax (which helps working people keep their money) and progressive income tax (which targets wealth, not workers). This creates what economists call "effective demand"—when working people have money, they spend it, which creates jobs and helps everyone.

Through Grainbank (an open-source currency system built on Grain OS), Oregon can create $WAVE currency to fund a Job Guarantee program. Workers get paid $18-22 per hour (regionally adjusted), with benefits including healthcare, childcare, and retirement. The program funds itself through currency creation, not taxation.

Now, there's an important constitutional consideration here. The U.S. Constitution prohibits states from coining money or emitting bills of credit. But here's what changes the equation: modern digital networking technology, combined with the federal government's ability to create dollars, means the federal sector can always outbid the private sector in any market for Oregon's currency. This isn't a threat to federal authority—it's a feature. The federal government maintains its position as the most trustworthy financial institution in the world, with unmatched stability in redeeming government bonds for ordinary living expenses. When state economies strengthen and liquidity improves, the value of the dollar rises—both domestically and in international markets. What works in Oregon can be replicated in other states, creating a competitive governance model that ultimately strengthens the dollar's position globally. Currency markets and exchanges don't weaken federal authority—they create healthy competition that makes the entire system more resilient.

This isn't theory. It's how money actually works. The question isn't whether we can do it—it's whether we have the political will to do it.

### Factory Farm Conversion: From Destruction to Regeneration

Here's a concrete proposal: Oregon uses MMT-generated dollars to purchase non-vegan animal factory farms and convert them into organic vegan permaculture food forests. This does several things at once:

**Environmental Restoration**: Factory farms are environmental disasters. They pollute water, degrade soil, and contribute to climate change. Converting them to permaculture food forests reverses this damage. We restore soil health, improve water quality, and sequester carbon.

**Animal Welfare**: Factory farms cause immense suffering. Converting them eliminates this suffering. We transition to plant-based agriculture that doesn't require animal exploitation.

**Economic Opportunity**: The conversion process creates Job Guarantee work. People learn permaculture, sustainable agriculture, and ecological restoration. They build skills that are valuable and transferable.

**Food Security**: Permaculture food forests produce diverse, nutritious food. They're more resilient than monoculture agriculture. They provide food security for communities while respecting the land and the creatures that call it home.

**Regional Optimization**: Oregon has diverse regions—West Cascades (Portland, Salem, Eugene) and East Cascades (Bend, Ashland, Rogue River, Klamath Falls). Each region has different climates, soils, and opportunities. We can optimize food forests for each region:

- **West Cascades**: Willamette Valley food forests with hemp, bamboo, timber, and diverse fruit and nut trees. Higher density, urban agriculture integration.

- **East Cascades**: High desert permaculture with drought-resistant plants, native grains, and sustainable timber. Lower density, larger agricultural buffers.

- **Additional Regions**: Coast (aquaculture integration), Columbia Gorge (wind energy, fruit orchards), Central Oregon (high desert agriculture, renewable energy).

The conversion process follows principles from Helen Atthowe's Ecological Farm: no-till agriculture, cover crops, compost and biochar, native plant integration, and pollinator habitat creation. We work with the land, not against it.

### Traditional Urbanism: Building Communities That Last

Traditional urbanism isn't about nostalgia. It's about building communities that are sustainable, beautiful, and human-scaled. It's about using materials that are local, renewable, and durable: wood, earth, brick, and stone instead of steel and glass.

**Walled Communities**: Walls define community boundaries. They create sustainable limits—not just physical boundaries, but limits on population, resource use, and growth. They create a natural hierarchy: main streets vs. alleys, gardens vs. squares, orchards vs. open fields.

**Walkable Districts**: Narrow streets, mixed materials (stone, gravel, dirt, tarmac), traffic calming, mixed use (pedestrians, bicycles, occasional vehicles). Human-scaled, not car-scaled.

**Water Integration**: Canals and drains throughout neighborhoods. Bioswales and rain gardens. Aquaculture in larger canals. Graywater systems for irrigation. Water isn't just infrastructure—it's part of the community's life.

**Productive Landscapes**: Espaliered fruit trees on south-facing walls. Community orchards. Pollarded trees for fuel and feed. Market gardens within town walls. Native landscaping inspired by "Botany Pays but Crime Doesn't" (a YouTube channel focused on indigenous landscaping that supports healthy native insects and pollinators).

**Building Design**: Multi-story buildings with ground-floor commercial spaces and residential above. Ground floors include: sustainable retail (local businesses), vegan restaurants, art and music venues, coworking clubs, tea bars, and poetry lounges. Upper floors: residential units (500-1,200 sq ft) with private bathrooms, gigabit fiber ethernet, natural ventilation, passive solar design, and balconies with espalier opportunities.

**Sustainable Materials**: Timber frame construction with mass timber (CLT, glulam) from sustainable forestry. Rammed earth walls and compressed earth blocks (CEB) from local soil. Fired brick from local clay. Fieldstone and cut stone from local quarries. Everything is local, renewable, and durable.

### GrainOS Technology: Power to the People

Technology should serve people, not the other way around. GrainOS is an open-source operating system designed for repairability, privacy, and local control. It's the foundation for a different kind of technology infrastructure.

**Local Compute Clusters**: Self-hosted, sustainable-energy-powered compute clusters (solar, wind, hydro) running GrainOS. These clusters provide local internet infrastructure, educational platforms, community governance (Constitution DAO), local business networks, and arts and culture platforms.

**Autonomous Farming Robotics**: Inspired by farm-ng's Amiga (a self-assemblable, open-source farming assistant robot), we integrate networked autonomous robotics and sensors for farming. These robots are designed to be no-till compatible—lightweight, precise, and respectful of soil health. They handle seeding, planting, weeding (precision, no herbicides), harvesting (selective, gentle), soil monitoring, and pollinator support.

**Job Guarantee Integration**: Workers learn robotics and automation, maintenance and repair, programming and optimization, swarm coordination management, and data analysis. Technology creates jobs, not eliminates them.

**Privacy and Control**: Your data stays yours. No tracking, no selling. Local control, not corporate control. Open-source, not proprietary. Repairable, not disposable.

## Part 3: Regional Strategy—Oregon's Diverse Opportunities

Oregon isn't one place. It's many places, each with unique characteristics, opportunities, and challenges. Our strategy must reflect this diversity.

### West Cascades: Portland, Salem, Eugene

**Characteristics**: Higher population density, existing infrastructure strong, urban/suburban transition zones, Willamette Valley agricultural land.

**Opportunities**: Factory farms in Willamette Valley (conversion targets), Portland metro (multiple satellite communities), Salem (state capital integration), Eugene (university town, progressive base).

**Housing Strategy**: Higher density traditional urbanism, multiple small walled communities, strong public transit integration, fiber internet already extensive.

**Agriculture Strategy**: Willamette Valley food forests, hemp/bamboo/timber (fast-growing), urban agriculture integration, community-supported agriculture (CSA) networks.

### East Cascades: Bend, Ashland, Rogue River, Klamath Falls

**Characteristics**: Lower population density, high desert climate, existing agricultural infrastructure, tourism economy integration.

**Opportunities**: Large-scale factory farm conversions, high desert permaculture (drought-resistant), timber industry transition, tourism + agriculture hybrid economy.

**Housing Strategy**: Lower density, more spread out, walled communities with larger agricultural buffers, solar/wind energy integration, water conservation focus.

**Agriculture Strategy**: High desert permaculture (agave, mesquite, native grains), timber from sustainable forestry, rammed earth construction materials, water-efficient food forests.

### Additional Regions

**Coast** (Astoria, Newport, Coos Bay): Ocean resilience focus, aquaculture integration, tourism + agriculture.

**Columbia Gorge** (Hood River, The Dalles): Wind energy integration, fruit tree orchards (existing), tourism economy.

**Central Oregon** (Prineville, Redmond): Data center transition opportunities, high desert agriculture, renewable energy infrastructure.

Each region has existing infrastructure connections: public power grid, water and sewage systems, and fiber internet. We build on what's already there, enhancing it rather than replacing it.
