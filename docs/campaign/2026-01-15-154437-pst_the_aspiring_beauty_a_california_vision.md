## Part Two: The Currency of Possibility

### How Money Actually Works

Let's talk about money for a minute, because this is where things get really interesting. There's an important, relatively new school of philosophy—actually, it's a school of thought about economics, and people working together, about people working together in present-day times, called contemporary Modern Monetary Theory, or also called Modern Money Theory, or even just M M T, for short. There are many economists who have been studying this for decades, but two really important names behind this theory are Randy Wray, and Stephanie Kelton, and they have been collaborators! Randy has some great lectures on YouTube, and Stephanie authored an amazing book, which is available on Spotify and Audible as audiobook as well as purchasable in physical or ebook form, she titled her book: 'The Deficit Myth'.

We're really scared about the national deficit, the financial deficit, the U.S. national debt, how much we've been told that the United States federal government owes and may never possibly be able to pay back. All sides of the political spectrum and media have been making us feel scared about this, almost all of our lives, even since we were little kids. But here's the thing, this is what Randy's lectures, and Stephanie's talks and her book, wonderfully tell us: Modern Monetary Theory describes how money actually works, and here's the thing: this is how it's always worked. The real question isn't whether we can create money for public good—we absolutely can. The question is whether we have the political will to do it, and do we have the resources, meaning healthy work environments for all of us, and the physical renewable resources from Mother Earth, who we should love with all our hearts?

And California? California is perfect for this. We have the largest state economy in the nation. We have the innovation capacity that makes Silicon Valley famous. We have the political will, and we have communities that are ready to thrive with it. We have the potential for healthy work environments that honor every worker. And we have Mother Earth's abundant renewable resources—sunshine that powers solar energy, wind that turns turbines, water that flows through our rivers, rich soil that can grow food forests, and a coastline that offers kelp and marine permaculture. California has everything we need to answer that question with a resounding yes.

Here's how it would work: California creates a state currency through an open-source currency system, built on open-source infrastructure. This funds a Job Guarantee program where workers earn minimum 25 dollars to 35 dollars an hour, adjusted regionally for California's diverse cost of living. The benefits? Healthcare, childcare, retirement—the basics that every family deserves. And the program funds itself through currency creation. It sounds complex, but really, it's about choosing to create money for public good.

The U.S. Constitution provides the framework, and modern digital networking technology combines with the federal government's ability to create dollars in ways that are fascinating. This means the federal sector can always support California's currency, which strengthens federal authority. The federal government stays the most trustworthy financial institution in the world, with unmatched stability. When state economies strengthen, liquidity improves, and the value of the dollar rises both domestically and internationally. What works in California can be replicated in other states, creating a competitive governance model that actually strengthens the dollar's position globally. Currency markets and exchanges strengthen federal authority by creating healthy competition that makes the entire system more resilient.

This is how the Federal Reserve creates money. This is how banks create money. We're just choosing to create it for public good—for jobs, for housing, for agriculture, for communities, for technology infrastructure, for education, for healthcare.

## Part Four: The Architecture of Community

### Traditional Urbanism: Beauty and Necessity

Traditional urbanism builds communities that are sustainable, beautiful, and human-scaled. It uses materials that are local, renewable, and durable: wood, earth, brick, and stone. It's a way of living together.

Think about walls. They define community boundaries and create sustainable limits—physical boundaries, limits on population, resource use, and growth. They also create a natural progression: main streets and alleys, gardens and squares, orchards and open fields. They define and create space.

Streets become community spaces—narrow, human-scaled, with mixed materials like stone, gravel, dirt, and tarmac. Traffic calming happens naturally through material variation. Mixed use means pedestrians, bicycles, and occasional vehicles all sharing the same space. The street becomes a place where neighbors meet, where children play, where community happens.

Water flows through canals and drains throughout neighborhoods. Bioswales and rain gardens catch and clean it. Aquatic plants grow in larger canals—watercress, lotus, water chestnuts, and other edible aquatic plants. Floating gardens and hydroponic systems turn water into food. Graywater systems irrigate gardens. Water becomes part of the community's life, its beauty, its productivity.

Trees are production. Espaliered fruit trees grow on south-facing walls. Community orchards feed everyone. Pollarded trees provide fuel and feed. Market gardens flourish within town walls. Native landscaping supports healthy native insects and pollinators. Trees mean productivity, beauty, and meaning, all at once.

Buildings rise multiple stories, with ground-floor commercial spaces and residential above. Ground floors host sustainable retail, plant-based restaurants, art and music venues, coworking spaces, tea houses, poetry reading rooms, community technology centers, open-source hardware shops. Upper floors hold residential units—500 to 1,200 square feet—with private bathrooms, high-speed fiber internet, natural ventilation, passive solar design, balconies with espalier opportunities.

Materials come from nearby: timber frame construction with mass timber from sustainable forestry, rammed earth walls and compressed earth blocks from local soil, fired brick from local clay, fieldstone and cut stone from local quarries. Everything is local, renewable, durable. Everything is permanent.

## Part Seven: The Implementation

### The Legislative Framework

This vision requires legislative action. Five bills, structured carefully, proposed strategically, each one building on the others.

**Bill One: The Job Guarantee Act** establishes the Job Guarantee program, creates state currency through open-source infrastructure, funds the program through currency creation. It sets wage rates at minimum 25 dollars to 35 dollars an hour, regionally adjusted for California's diverse cost of living, and establishes benefits: healthcare, childcare, retirement.

**Bill Two: The Farm Conversion Act** authorizes purchase of farms, establishes conversion to vegan permaculture food forests, creates Job Guarantee work opportunities. It sets environmental standards: no-till, organic, native plants. It establishes regional optimization for Central Valley, Bay Area, Los Angeles Basin, Inland Empire, Central Coast, North Coast, Sierra Nevada, and High Desert.

**Bill Three: The Traditional Urbanism Housing Act** authorizes traditional urbanism housing communities, establishes sustainable materials requirements, creates walkable and mixed-use districts, sets density and design standards, and integrates with the Job Guarantee program.

**Bill Four: The Technology Integration Act** establishes fair-trade infrastructure with local compute cluster systems, creates sustainable energy requirements, authorizes autonomous farming robotics, sets data privacy and local control standards, requires open-source software and hardware where possible, ensures fair compensation for technology workers, and integrates with the Job Guarantee program.

**Bill Five: The Education Innovation Act** authorizes open-source-inspired homeschooling programs, establishes community education centers, creates intergenerational learning opportunities, sets multicultural and spiritual enrichment standards, and integrates with the Job Guarantee program.
